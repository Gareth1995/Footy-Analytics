Football Player Injury Risk Analysis

Football
Author

Gareth Edwards

May 25, 2026

Listen, football injuries aren’t cool. I’ve elborated on this in depth in my previous blog post so let me not bore you with further ramblings. I want to get down to the meat (pause).

We know how critical injury prevention is for a Premier League team who want to win the league. The average number of gameweeks missed per player for a team closely correlates with where that team finishes in the season, and this is especially true for the top sides in England. The margins are extremely fine. The plot below shows us that teams who maintain lower average gameweeks missed per player are far more likely to finish in the top two.

The question now begs, how can a team prevent these injuries from happening? The answer may be found in survival analysis. Survival analysis models time-to-event and has the ability to convert this to a risk score over time. It helps the coaching staff and physio staff to know, at a given time, what is the injury risk score of player x.

The approach I took was to loosely follow the study by Catrell et al (Catterall et al. 2026). Here a Survival Analysis was carried out on the SoccerMon dataset (Midoglu et al. 2022). SoccerMon is “A Large-Scale Multivariate Soccer Athlete Health, Performance, and Position Monitoring Dataset”. SoccerMon is the largest soccer athlete dataset available that tracks both subjective (using questionnaire methods) and objective features (using GPS trackers and other sensors). The data comes from two teams from the Norwegian women’s elite soccer league over the span of two years (2020-2021).

I used this paper to inform which features I extract from the SoccerMon dataset. Below is a list of the features I extracted…

Each feature represents a 21 day moving average of that feature.

After extensive EDA, data splitting and the necessary transformations (StandardScaler for columns with a normal distribution and Yeo-Johnson scaling for skewed data). These transformations help the model see the patterns in the data better as it places all the numbers on the same scale. Without transformations any column with large numbers like Speed_km_h_mean compared to columns with smaller numbers like ACWR will dominate the model during training.

Two further features were added to the dataset, and that was event and time-to-event. Where event is a boolean value (0 or 1) denoting if a player got injured within the next seven days (1) or not (0). Time-to-event was the number of days leading up to the event (starting at 7 days back). See an example of the unscaled dataset below:

So, how do we use these features to inform us whether a player is at a high risk of injury or not? Well, this is where Survival Analysis comes into play. For this problem I have used the Random Forest Survival model and trained it on the features we see above using a leave one player out process. This means the model was trained on every player except one and then the trained model was applied to that one player to mimic a real world application. This process was repeated with each player.

The results are promising. Look at the plot below!

These plots visualise how the risk value corresponds with actual injury periods. Before each injury there is a peak in risk. This can serve as an incredibly valuable tool in managing player load and injury prevention. Now one may note that there are periods of increased injury risk yet no injury takes place. This is just the nature of the sport, a player may be teetering on the edge but due to luck or good, intuitive, player management by the coach, that player was spared an injury and missing those all too important gameweek days.
